Fair Value Gaps: The Ultimate Trading Guide

Published March 2026 · 9 min read

If there's one concept that changed how retail traders approach the market, it's the Fair Value Gap (FVG). It's one of the most reliable entry tools in Smart Money trading — and once you understand why they form, you'll see them everywhere.

What Is a Fair Value Gap?

A Fair Value Gap is a three-candle pattern where the middle candle creates an imbalance — a gap between the wicks of the first and third candles where no trading occurred.

In simpler terms: price moved so aggressively that it left a "gap" in the price action. The market tends to come back and fill this gap before continuing in the original direction.

Bullish FVG

  • Candle 1: Any candle
  • Candle 2: Strong bullish candle (the displacement)
  • Candle 3: Any candle
  • The gap: Between Candle 1's high and Candle 3's low

If Candle 3's low is higher than Candle 1's high, that space between them is the FVG. This is a buying zone — when price retraces down to fill this gap, it's a potential long entry.

Bearish FVG

  • Candle 1: Any candle
  • Candle 2: Strong bearish candle (the displacement)
  • Candle 3: Any candle
  • The gap: Between Candle 1's low and Candle 3's high

If Candle 3's high is lower than Candle 1's low, that space is the FVG. This is a selling zone — when price retraces up to fill this gap, it's a potential short entry.

Why Do FVGs Form?

FVGs form because of institutional aggression. When smart money enters the market with heavy volume, price moves so fast that normal two-way trading doesn't occur. The result is an imbalance that the market naturally wants to correct.

Think of it like a rubber band: the more it stretches (the bigger the displacement), the stronger the pullback tendency.

How to Trade FVGs

Step 1: Identify HTF Bias

Before looking for FVGs, determine the higher timeframe direction. A bullish FVG in a bearish market is likely to get blown through. Only trade FVGs that align with the trend.

Step 2: Identify the FVG

On your entry timeframe (5m or 1m), find the three-candle pattern with a clear gap between Candle 1 and Candle 3 wicks. The bigger the displacement candle (Candle 2), the stronger the FVG.

Step 3: Wait for Price to Return

Don't chase. Let price come back to the FVG zone. This is the retracement. Most traders enter too early — patience is the edge.

Step 4: Look for Confirmation

When price enters the FVG, look for a reaction: a rejection candle, a shift in lower timeframe structure, or displacement in the opposite direction. This confirms the FVG is being respected.

Step 5: Enter with Defined Risk

Enter on the confirmation. Stop loss goes beyond the FVG (below for bullish, above for bearish). Target the next liquidity pool or the high/low that the original displacement was targeting.

What Is an Inverse FVG (IFVG)?

An Inverse FVG is a regular FVG that has been mitigated (price returned and traded through it). Once an FVG is "used up," it can flip and act as support or resistance in the opposite direction.

  • A bullish FVG that gets filled and broken becomes a bearish IFVG (resistance)
  • A bearish FVG that gets filled and broken becomes a bullish IFVG (support)

IFVGs are advanced, but they're powerful. When a level that was supposed to hold as support breaks and flips to resistance, trapped traders on the wrong side add fuel to the move.

FVG Trading Rules

  1. Only trade FVGs that align with HTF bias. Bullish FVGs in a bullish market, bearish FVGs in a bearish market.
  2. Bigger displacement = stronger FVG. A tiny gap from a small candle isn't worth trading.
  3. FVGs on higher timeframes are more significant. A 1-hour FVG is stronger than a 1-minute FVG.
  4. Not every FVG gets filled. If price never comes back, there's no trade. Move on.
  5. Partially filled FVGs still work. Price doesn't have to fill the entire gap — sometimes it reacts from the 50% level (consequent encroachment).
  6. Combine with other confluence. An FVG that overlaps with an order block or a key level is significantly more reliable.

Common FVG Mistakes

  • Trading every FVG you see: Most FVGs on a 1-minute chart are noise. Focus on the ones that align with structure and HTF bias.
  • No stop loss: "The FVG should hold" — until it doesn't. Always define your risk.
  • Entering without confirmation: Price touching the FVG isn't enough. Wait for a reaction.
  • Ignoring context: An FVG during London or New York session is more reliable than one at 2 AM.
